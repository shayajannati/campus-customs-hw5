Customer order

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee, size S

  • A customer asked for 1 Classic Bulldog Tee (CC-TEE-WHITE) in size S.
  • The database shows size S out of stock (0 on hand), list price $28.
  • The ticket links vendor invoice 501: $840 from Bulldog Print Co for a rush reprint of this tee in size S, due 2026-08-28, status open, which is 3 days overdue at the business date (2026-08-31). The payments table has no rows.

Expected Planned handling (a plan, not an execution result)

1. First specialist Boss should call

Inventory The order cannot move until we know whether the item exists to sell. Inventory confirms the size S shortage and which other sizes are in stock. That fact decides whether the invoice question matters at all, so it comes before any money analysis.

2. Anticipated agent-to-agent delegations

FromToReason
Boss→InventoryFirst call: stock facts for the requested tee and size.
Inventory→AccountingThe shortage is tied to an overdue reprint invoice. Inventory cannot judge invoices, vendors or payment records, so it hands the invoice 501 question to Accounting.

Not called: Facilities (no rent issue) and Customer Service (nothing is safe to promise yet; a human decides first).

3. Expected MCP tools

  • get_ticket_context BossBoss reads the ticket and its linked stock, price and invoice.
  • get_business_date BossAnchor every overdue judgment to the store's date, not the computer's.
  • check_product_stock InventoryStock by size for CC-TEE-WHITE, including size S.
  • get_invoice_status AccountingAmount, due date, days past due and any matching payment for invoice 501.
  • get_vendor_info AccountingVendor lead time for the reprint supplier.

Payment-record uncertainty is covered by get_invoice_status, which reports any matching payment and flags that the matching rule is unverified. Accounting also holds get_payment_history and could call it, but the plan does not depend on it. get_business_date is available to every specialist.

4. Anticipated recommendation and escalation

  • Size S is out of stock. Do not promise shipment, a restock date or a reservation while invoice 501 is unresolved.
  • Escalate to a human: whether to pay invoice 501 and whether to order a reprint. Paying an $840 invoice for a $28 sale needs human judgment.
  • Accounting reports payment-record uncertainty plainly: no payment is recorded, which does not prove the invoice is unpaid outside the database.
  • Offer other sizes only as an option for a human or Customer Service to propose later, not as a commitment.

Reflection

I started this project wanting to know whether I could trust a small team of AI agents with a shop's real decisions. All three tickets ended with the status “resolved”, but in this reflection “resolved” means only that the agent workflow completed. It does not mean the underlying business issue was solved: invoice 501 ($840) is still unpaid, two stock shortages are unfilled, no discount was approved, and the only cash that moved was one rent payment that I approved myself.

Question 1: How I evaluate the agents on each ticket

I did not just ask whether the agents were right. I asked whether they stayed in their lane, whether they sent the right decisions to a person, and what it cost to get there. On that standard the agents were careful but not efficient.

Ticket 101 (Bulldog tee, size S)

  • What they got right. They found size S at 0 on hand and invoice 501 at $840, 3 days past due. Accounting reminded me that an empty payments table does not prove nothing was paid outside the system. Customer Service wrote a reply with the $28.00 list price that promised nothing about a restock. Boss escalated three separate decisions: pay the invoice, authorize a reprint, and make any promise to the customer.
  • A judgment call that impressed me. The team noticed an $840 reprint bill against a $28 sale and said that needs a person's judgment.
  • What I would criticize. Inventory made one tool call, get_ticket_context, which Boss had already made, and that call already contains the stock and the shortfall. So that delegation mostly repeated what Boss knew.
  • Cost. 11 model requests, 13 tool calls, 31,244 tokens, about 41 seconds.

Ticket 102 (rent)

  • What they got right. Facilities confirmed the lease at $2,400 due 2026-09-02, two days out and not overdue. It also saw that the email note names no landlord and no amount, so those claims “cannot be compared”. That was the instinct I wanted: the lease is the authority, not an email. Accounting added that paying rent alone would leave $1,000, and rent plus invoice 501 would leave $160. Only one decision came to me: whether to pay.
  • What I would criticize. get_ticket_context ran three times (Boss, Facilities, Accounting) and get_business_date twice.
  • Cost. 9 requests, 10 tool calls, 25,037 tokens, about 50 seconds.
  • The human step. After the run I approved one simulated payment (payment 1). It is the only cash movement in the whole exercise, and the Cash tab shows it: $3,400.00 to $1,000.00.

Ticket 103 (Yale AI Club hoodies)

  • What they got right. 20 requested, 8 on hand, shortfall 12, $58 list, $22 cost. Accounting said to keep the $36 margin “as a verified fact only” and not to treat the cost as an approved discount floor. The customer draft has 8 and $58 and no cost, margin or cash figure.
  • What I would criticize. evaluate_bulk_order ran twice (Inventory and Accounting). Accounting also pulled the overdue invoice and rent into a discount ticket. That was useful context but a little off-topic, and it came from the Boss prompt's instruction to check cash, which I wrote.
  • The customer draft. It says “We're reviewing your bulk discount request and checking whether the remaining 12 can be fulfilled” and then, correctly, “we can't confirm the additional quantity or a delivery date yet.” I do not think this is a failure, because it states its own limits. But a customer could read “reviewing your discount” as a discount being considered, and “checking whether the remaining 12 can be fulfilled” as someone sourcing hoodies. Nobody has decided to do either. Every draft goes to a human before it is sent, so I call this a tone risk to edit, not a confirmed error.
  • Cost. 11 requests, 13 tool calls, 32,627 tokens, about 44 seconds.
The bigger point. Three workflows completed, but the business is mostly unresolved. Invoice 501 is still unpaid, the tee shortage and the 12-hoodie shortage are unfilled, and no discount was approved. In all three runs Boss's status was “needs human approval”. Only rent moved money. I rate the agents high on accuracy and restraint, and middling on efficiency.

What the hub meant for me as a manager

  • Accountability was clearer. Every task Boss gave is on the record, each report is attributed to one agent, and Boss owns the final recommendation. The flip side is that Boss's wording of each task shaped the answer. Accounting's task on 102 explicitly asked about “competing obligations”, and that is exactly what came back.
  • Coordination. Boss had the whole picture, which is how one summary could say rent plus invoice 501 leaves $160. But the specialists never used the ability to raise something with a colleague, so Facilities could not pull Accounting in on its own judgment. The loop and chain-length guards I built were never exercised.
  • Efficiency. Boss made about 4 of the 9 to 11 requests in each run (approximate, derived from cumulative usage), so roughly a third to a half of the model calls were coordination. On 101 and 103 Boss sent the Inventory and Accounting tasks in the same second and Customer Service about 19 and 15 seconds later, after it had their reports. On 102 the two delegations were about 14 seconds apart.

Question 3: What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

The honest answer is that the retrieval was the wasteful part, not the specialists. The same facts were fetched more than once in a run: get_ticket_context twice on 101 and 103 and three times on 102, get_business_date twice on 101 and 102, and evaluate_bulk_order twice on 103. That part would be simpler.

The roles are a different matter. A single agent holding every tool would also have to be trusted with unit cost while writing customer text. Customer Service shows why I would not merge it: its tools are get_customer_safe_ticket_context and check_product_stock, and the MCP server itself never returns the $22 cost to it. In Ticket 103 its draft had only 8 and $58. That protection is built into the permissions, not just the prompt. By contrast, Inventory on 101 and the split between Facilities and Accounting were more about organization than safety.

Rather than removing agents, I would try shared evidence first. Boss could run get_ticket_context and get_business_date once and pass the results to the specialists in the task, or the app could keep a per-run cache of read-only tool results keyed by tool and arguments. These are reads of the same database within seconds, so a cache is plausible. It would need clearing whenever something is written, such as a payment or a ticket resolving, because the ticket context includes payment state.

I cannot claim any version would be faster or cheaper. I never ran a single-agent baseline, and I do not know how much of each request's cost is the fixed instructions versus the tool results.

Why read-only tools and protected human approval mattered

No agent role has a write tool. The agents could recommend things, including a $2,400 payment, but the only thing that moved cash was payment 1, made through a protected route that needs an admin token and an approver name. It revalidates the amount against the database, blocks duplicates, and leaves an audit trail (payment_attempt 7b57f208fd1c and payment_committed 0c75ad7679ee). The agents' own warning that paying rent and invoice 501 together leaves $160 is exactly the tradeoff I want a person to decide. That separation is also why “resolved” on the ticket board is safe to show: it cannot be mistaken for permission to spend.

Limits of this evaluation

Each ticket ran once, so my judgments are not a benchmark. The per-agent request split and the elapsed times come from the audit trail and are approximate. I did not run a single-agent baseline or test the caching idea. The three solvable examples were checked by running the read-only tools on the current database, not by running the agents, so they describe what the team could do, not what it did.

Expected sections are the plans written before any run. Actual sections and the Cash tab come from the three genuine live runs, the audit trail and the working database. Workflow “resolved” means the agent run finished; it is not a business approval.
